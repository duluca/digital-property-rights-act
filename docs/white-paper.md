# Digital Property Rights Act

A narrow, passable proposal to make “buy” mean buy for consumer digital goods


## Executive summary

Property rights should not disappear simply because property becomes digital. The Digital Property Rights Act would create a narrow federal rule: when a seller uses consumer-facing words such as “buy,” “purchase,” “own,” or “sale” for a consumer digital good, the seller must deliver a usable copy that the purchaser can download, retain, back up, and use without ongoing seller authentication. The seller does not have to host, upgrade, patch, remaster, or support the copy forever unless separately promised.

The proposal deliberately avoids the hardest political traps. It does not mandate digital resale in the first bill. It does not require cloud games, MMOs, SaaS platforms, streaming services, or online worlds to become downloadable goods. It preserves copyright law, including prohibitions on public distribution and unauthorized reproduction. It simply restores the ordinary meaning of a sale.

## The narrow bill to pursue first

Truth-in-sale rule: “Buy,” “purchase,” “own,” and “sale” may be used only when ownership of a usable copy transfers.

Download/delivery rule: The seller must provide the digital good for download or equivalent delivery at purchase, or within a reasonable stated download window.

No perpetual hosting rule: delivery satisfies the seller unless the seller separately promises redownloads, cloud backup, upgrades, or continuing support.

Offline usability rule: a purchased copy may not require indefinite seller authentication, except for features clearly sold as online services.

Copyright preservation rule: ownership of the copy does not transfer copyright and does not authorize unlawful sharing, uploading, mass copying, or public distribution.

Service carve-out: streaming catalogs, SaaS, cloud gaming, MMOs, multiplayer servers, APIs, live databases, and generative AI services are services if clearly labeled as such.

Safe harbor: a DRM-free or offline-usable copy, delivered in a reasonable download window with plain-language disclosures, satisfies the Act.

## Why not lead with resale?

Digital resale is philosophically attractive but legally and politically harder because resale of a file can implicate the copyright reproduction right. The ReDigi litigation shows the risk: even a service designed to move files while deleting the seller’s copy was held to create infringing reproductions. Therefore, the first bill should establish possession, retention, backup, and offline use. Resale should be framed as a study, pilot safe harbor, or second-phase rule once ownership is recognized.

## Legal foundation

First sale already recognizes that owning a copy is different from owning the copyright. The gap is that digital transfers often create legally relevant reproductions, and software licenses can be drafted to avoid ownership treatment.

Consumer protection law already polices misleading sales language. California AB 2426 shows a live legislative path by requiring clearer disclosure when digital goods are licensed rather than owned. The federal Act should build on that by creating a real ownership option.

The Act is compatible with copyright because it regulates consumer-facing sales language and copy delivery while preserving the copyright owner’s exclusive rights against unauthorized reproduction and distribution.

## Definitions

Consumer digital good: a fixed digital file or package of files offered as a discrete product to consumers, including audiovisual works, music, ebooks, applications, downloadable games, installers, fonts, digital art, 3D models, and course files.

Digital service: an ongoing remote service whose essential value depends on continuing server-side operation, including streaming catalogs, cloud gaming, MMOs, SaaS, APIs, generative AI services, multiplayer servers, live databases, and social platforms.

Sale: a transaction transferring ownership of one usable copy of a consumer digital good.

Rental/subscription/access: time-limited or continuing access that does not transfer ownership and must be labeled in plain language before payment.

## Model statutory core

A person may not advertise, offer, or complete a consumer transaction using the terms “buy,” “purchase,” “own,” “sale,” or substantially similar language for a consumer digital good unless the transaction transfers ownership of one usable copy of that digital good to the purchaser.

Ownership of a consumer digital good includes the right to download or otherwise receive the copy, retain the copy indefinitely, store it locally or in personal cloud storage, make personal backup copies, migrate the copy to new storage media or compatible formats for personal use, and use the copy without ongoing dependence on the seller’s authentication servers, except for features clearly disclosed and sold as digital services.

Nothing in this Act transfers copyright ownership or authorizes public distribution, public performance, public display, circumvention for infringement, or reproduction beyond personal ownership uses otherwise permitted by law.

## Recommended ask to Congress

“Please ask House Judiciary staff to examine whether federal law should prohibit use of the words buy, purchase, own, or sale for consumer digital goods unless the seller delivers a usable copy that the consumer can download, retain, back up, and use without indefinite seller authentication. We are not asking for perpetual hosting, free upgrades, mandatory resale, or changes to piracy law.”

## Conclusion

The best first bill is not a universal digital property code. It is a narrow, intelligible rule against fake sales: if a company says buy, it must deliver a usable copy; if it sells access, it must say access. That is the version with the best chance of passing.
